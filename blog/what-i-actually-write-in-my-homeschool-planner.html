What I Actually Write in My Homeschool Planner

September 17, 2026 · Mom Life

HomeSchoolFTS -- homeschoolfts.com

A real look inside the planner of a working homeschool parent.

I have seen homeschool planners on Pinterest. They are beautiful -- color-coded, hand-lettered, with stickers and washi tape and intricate weekly spreads that look like they took an hour to fill out.

My planner does not look like that.

For years I thought it should. I bought the beautiful planners, sat down with my fine-tip pens, and tried to fill them out the way the Instagram accounts showed me. I would spend a Sunday afternoon on it and feel productive. Then Monday would arrive and my carefully planned schedule would collapse by 10am, and the beautiful planner would make me feel guilty instead of organized.

Now I use a spiral notebook. Here is exactly what I write in it.

What Goes on the Weekly Spread

The Three Goals

At the top of every week, I write three goals. Not ten. Not a full list. Three.

These are the non-negotiable things I want to accomplish this week in our homeschool. Not "finish chapter 4 in the math book" -- more like "make sure we do not skip writing two days in a row again" or "get through the next two history lessons" or "do the science experiment we have been putting off."

If those three things happen, the week was a success. Everything else is a bonus.

Where We Are

Below the goals, I write one line per subject with just enough context to remember where we are:

  • Math: halfway through multiplication unit. He is getting it.
  • Reading: finished Charlotte's Web. She wants to read another EB White.
  • History: starting the Civil War chapter this week.
  • Science: need to do the plant experiment we set up last week.
  • Writing: she owes me one rough draft from last week.

This is not a plan. It is a status update for my own brain. It takes about five minutes.

The Schedule Reality

I write down any appointments, activities, or commitments that will affect our school time: co-op on Tuesday, dentist on Thursday afternoon, visiting grandparent on Wednesday. These are the fixed points around which everything else flows.

I do not write a schedule. I write constraints.

What Goes on the Daily Page

Most days I write nothing in advance. At the start of the school day, I open to a blank page and jot down:

  1. Today's most important lesson or task
  2. Any materials I need to remember to get out
  3. A note if anyone has something going on emotionally that I need to be aware of ("she woke up anxious" or "he had a rough night")

At the end of the day, I write a quick record:

  • What we actually did (not what I planned -- what actually happened)
  • Anything worth noting about how it went
  • Anything that needs to carry over to tomorrow

This end-of-day record is the most valuable part of my whole planning system. It takes three minutes and becomes my paper trail. When I look back at a month of entries, I can see patterns: what subjects we keep skipping, which kids are thriving and which are struggling, what formats are working.

What I Never Write Anymore

Timed blocks. Lesson X from 9:00 to 9:45. Never again. The moment something goes over time, everything feels late, and I spend the rest of the day trying to catch up on paper instead of teaching.

Long to-do lists. If I write twelve things I want to accomplish in a day, we will do five and I will feel like I failed. I pick three important things and call that a good day.

Assignments I am not sure we will actually do. I used to write optimistic lesson plans full of extension activities and extra reading and projects I hoped we would get to. Now I plan the minimum and let the good days surprise me.

The Real Purpose of the Planner

My planner is not for showing anyone else. It is not a record for a school board or an accountability partner. It is a tool for my own brain.

It holds what I cannot keep in my head: where we are, what matters most, what we cannot forget. It releases the mental load so I can be present with my kids during school instead of anxiously trying to remember what comes next.

The most useful thing in my planner is not the schedule. It is the record of what actually happened -- because that record tells the truth about our homeschool in a way that any plan never can.
