5 Free Apps That Actually Help You Stay Organized as a Homeschool Parent

September 19, 2026 · Smart Living

Homeschooling comes with a weird amount of stuff to keep track of.

Lessons. Appointments. Library books. Activities. Grocery lists. Assignments. Random websites you swear you will remember later. That field trip form sitting somewhere in your email.

And somehow, dinner still has to happen.

I have learned that being organized does not mean having the perfect color-coded system. It means having somewhere to put all the stuff floating around in your head so you are not trying to remember everything at once.

The good news is you do not need another expensive homeschool subscription to do it. These five apps all have free options that can actually help with real homeschool life.

Best for: Keeping everyone's schedule in one place

1. Google Calendar

Google Calendar is probably the least exciting app on this list, but honestly, it might be the most useful.

You can create separate calendars for homeschool, appointments, activities, work, family events, or even individual kids. It also works well for reminders and tasks when you need one place to see what is coming up.

For homeschooling, I would use it for:

  • Co-op days
  • Field trips
  • Doctor appointments
  • Assignment deadlines
  • Library due dates
  • Testing dates
  • Sports and activities
  • Mom's own appointments and deadlines

One thing I really like about separate calendars is that you can turn them on and off. If you only want to see homeschool stuff for a minute, you can.

FTS Tip: Give each child their own calendar color, then create another calendar for things that involve the whole family.

Best for: Visual homeschool planning

2. Trello

If you are someone who likes moving things around visually, Trello makes a lot of sense.

Trello uses boards, lists, and cards. Think of it like digital sticky notes that do not fall behind your desk.

You could create a homeschool board with four simple lists:

To Do | This Week | Working On | Finished

Then each assignment becomes a card. You could also make boards for curriculum planning, projects, field trip ideas, books to read, or resources you want to try.

This can work especially well for older kids because they can actually see work moving from To Do to Finished. And yes, moving something into the Finished column is weirdly satisfying.

FTS Tip: Do not build a giant 47-column homeschool command center on day one. Start with three or four lists. You can always add more later.

Best for: Running the whole family, not just homeschool

3. Cozi Family Organizer

Cozi is made specifically for families, which makes it a little different from the other apps here.

Its free tools can help keep family calendars, lists, meals, and everyday responsibilities in one shared place. That means homeschool can live next to all the other things that somehow become Mom's responsibility.

You could use Cozi for:

  • Family appointments
  • Grocery lists
  • Chores
  • Activities
  • Meal planning
  • Homeschool events
  • Shared family reminders

This one makes sense if your biggest problem is not necessarily planning lessons. It is coordinating everything else around those lessons.

Because a beautifully planned science lesson does not help much when you suddenly remember somebody has a dentist appointment at 1:30.

FTS Tip: This is a good option if you want one family system that your kids or partner can also check instead of everyone asking you what is happening today.

Best for: Keeping homeschool information together

4. Notion

Notion can be as simple or as complicated as you make it.

That is both the good part and the dangerous part.

You can use pages, databases, checklists, tables, notes, and templates to build your own homeschool hub. You could keep curriculum links, lesson plans, reading lists, homeschool records, project ideas, educational websites, notes about what worked, and notes about what absolutely did not work.

I especially like the idea of using Notion as a place to save all those homeschool resources you find online.

Because bookmarking 600 websites and telling yourself you will remember why you saved them is apparently not an organizational system.

FTS Tip: Start with one page called Homeschool Hub. Add sections for each child, useful links, curriculum, and things you want to try. Stop there. You do not need to become a Notion architect before breakfast.

Best for: Getting the endless list out of your head

5. Todoist

Sometimes you do not need another planner. You just need somewhere to dump everything you suddenly remember.

Todoist is great for that. Its free plan gives you a simple task system without requiring you to build an entire productivity universe first.

You could create projects like:

  • Homeschool
  • Home
  • Kids
  • HomeSchoolFTS
  • Personal

Then when you suddenly remember "order printer ink," "find that science worksheet," "return library books," or "email the co-op," you put it in Todoist instead of expecting your brain to keep holding onto it while you are also explaining multiplication.

That alone can make life feel less chaotic.

FTS Tip: Use Todoist as your inbox, not another place you have to perfectly organize. Write the task down first. Sort it later if you need to.

Which One Should You Use?

Here is the thing. You probably should not use all five. That would completely defeat the purpose.

  • If your biggest problem is your schedule, start with Google Calendar.
  • If you are a visual planner, try Trello.
  • If your whole family schedule feels chaotic, look at Cozi.
  • If you need one place for homeschool information and resources, try Notion.
  • If your brain constantly has 900 unfinished tasks bouncing around inside it, Todoist might be the best starting point.

Pick the tool that solves the problem you actually have, not the one with the prettiest social media setup.

A Tool Should Make Homeschooling Easier

I am a big believer that organization should support your homeschool, not become another homeschool project.

You do not need the perfect app. You do not need to spend an entire weekend building a complicated productivity system. And you definitely do not need to pay for five different subscriptions just to remember that library books are due Thursday.

Start small.

Choose one tool. Give it a real job. Use it for a couple of weeks.

If it makes your life easier, keep it. If it creates more work than it saves, delete it.

That is the whole point of Smart Living here at HomeSchoolFTS. Find the tools that actually help, skip the ones that do not, and build systems that work for your real life.
